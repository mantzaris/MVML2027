Evidence-Grounded Generative AI for Underwater Acoustic Reasoning

Anonymous author

Abstract — Underwater acoustic operators need to identify sound sources, inspect the supporting evidence and recognize when an automated answer needs review. We present an evidence-centred approach to applying agentic and generative AI to these decisions. Two linked studies on real recordings make tool selection, vessel-category identification and generated answers separately testable. In a 100-recording reporting study, learned selection uses 49.5% fewer logical tool queries than a fixed heuristic and reduces wrong or unsupported details from nine to one, while revealing a loss of supported information from 53 details to 37. On 235 previously unused recordings from 218 source groups, evidence fusion achieves 60.4% category accuracy versus 55.3% for a single acoustic model. Removing measured acoustic features lowers macro-F1 from 0.583 to 0.517 in an exploratory comparison; the primary macro-F1 test does not establish superiority to a three-model ensemble. Across 705 generated answers, fused evidence yields 44 wrong labels versus 68 with acoustic-prior prompting, with 99 correct answers in both conditions and 24 more withheld. Tests with six recording changes and 72 clips from unfamiliar vessel categories yield a design finding: the system can issue more answers as noise increases even while correctness declines. These findings identify practical design priorities: make evidence inspectable, protect category decisions during explanation, and assess errors together with withheld information. The contribution is a reproducible methodology and application study for developing acoustic AI assistance, evaluated using dataset labels and automated checks; effects on human operators remain to be tested.

Keywords: underwater acoustics; vessel identification; evidence fusion; selective classification; generative audio; operator support

1. Introduction and Related Work

An acoustic operator must decide what produced a sound and whether the available evidence justifies that decision. Confusing a cargo vessel with a tanker, overlooking an unfamiliar vessel type, or accepting a confident but incorrect explanation can each undermine the usefulness of an automated assistant. Existing passive-acoustic software supports sound displays, classification and review [1]. Agentic AI offers a further possibility: choosing which evidence to inspect before answering. Realizing that possibility requires measuring what its decisions add, what they get wrong and what useful information they withhold.

We introduce an application-specific methodology that connects observable AI decisions to these practical outcomes. Two linked studies examine adaptive acoustic-tool selection and, separately, evidence-based vessel identification followed by generative reporting. The contributions are an inspectable evidence-acquisition loop, controlled tests on previously unused recorded sources, and an accounting of correct, incorrect and withheld information. This makes it possible to assess an assistant even when a fluent explanation or a high confidence score is misleading. Here identification means assigning a vessel category to a five-second recording; individual vessel identity and location are outside the task.

The approach builds on established methods while testing their value for acoustic assistance. Underwater feature fusion combines complementary sound measurements [2], and SelectiveNet jointly learns classification and rejection [3]. Our referral rule uses confidence and disagreement between evidence sources; it does not inherit SelectiveNet’s guarantees. UniqueShip motivates separating recorded sources during evaluation [4]. We therefore compare against both a strong three-model ensemble and a model trained on the same additional labels, and count every recording that receives no automatic answer.

AuTAgent learns when and which audio tools to use [5]; Audio-Mind, SEAR and ACE-Cap examine auditable or adaptive acoustic evidence [6–8]. From Signals to Evidence Parts I and II study underwater interpretation using simulated vessel sources and propagation [9,10]; these and [6–8] are preprints in our source record. Our distinctive application study connects evidence requests, measured sound characteristics, category decisions and generated overrides on real recordings. Its focus is the information that reaches a reviewer under noise and unfamiliar categories. Citation support, correctness and completeness are evaluated as separate properties [11].

2. Motivation, Data and the Fresh Evaluation

The first study tests how an agent obtains evidence. A learned neural controller chooses spectral content (which frequencies are present), modulation (repeating changes in loudness), temporal/quality measures or similar training recordings. It updates its state after each query and stops after at most two queries for Omni to generate a cited report. Unqueried results are hidden. Cached tool cards and terminal generations permit controlled replay; query counts describe logical acquisition, not measured runtime savings. On 100 clips, the controller uses 101 queries versus the heuristic’s 200, with fewer wrong details but less supported information (Fig. 3A–B). This finding motivates the second study: testing whether evidence helps identify the source category itself.

We use UniqueShip v1.0, fold 1 of the five-hour-per-class release, retaining background, cargo, passenger, tanker and tug/tow. The original acquired recordings are mono PCM at 32 kHz and five seconds long. Five duration mismatches and 42 filename/metadata date conflicts were excluded. Original partitions separate recorded vessels, background UTC-day groups and entire UTC days across all classes/hydrophones, with a global 60-second gap between clips in different partitions. The acoustic backbone and prototype bank use acoustic-fit only; the follow-up heads use policy-fit; development selects settings and calibration fixes final reporting thresholds.

3. Evidence-Based Category Identification

The acoustic classifier is a fixed Swin V2 Tiny [12], trained with random seed 17. It reads a time–frequency image of each recording: 128 log-mel bands over 20–8,000 Hz from anti-aliased 20 kHz audio (1,024-sample window/FFT, 256-sample hop). A 90 dB relative range is resized to 256×256, replicated across three channels and ImageNet-normalized. Training uses AdamW, batch 32, head rate 0.001, weight decay 0.01, up to 30 epochs and patience five. Development selects backbone rate 10⁻⁵ from {10⁻⁶,10⁻⁵,3×10⁻⁵}; seeds 29 and 43 complete the ensemble. Fresh recordings never train these models.

The matched acoustic-only model uses 32 principal-component coordinates (PCA, a compact representation of the learned sound features) and five log category probabilities. PCA uses acoustic-fit data only. The comparison ensemble averages separately calibrated probabilities from three independently trained models. Fusion instead uses one model plus explicit measurements. This comparison tests the value of adding evidence; it does not measure online runtime.

The 62 measured features describe frequency content, repeating amplitude changes and recording quality. A Welch power spectrum (8,192-sample window, 4,096 overlap) supplies 32 relative band powers over 20–8,000 Hz, four broad-band energy fractions and six descriptors of spectral shape and signal peaks. Four temporal/quality values describe first/last-second energy change, quarter-second loudness variability, zeros and digital clipping. Sixteen modulation values describe relative envelope-spectrum energy in eight intervals from 2 to 80 Hz for each of two carrier bands. These are relative recording measurements; no calibrated sound pressure is inferred.

Modulation extraction downsamples to 8 kHz with anti-aliasing, filters 100–1,000 and 1,000–3,500 Hz with fourth-order filters, removes 0.25 s Hilbert-envelope edges and resamples envelopes to 400 Hz. These are received modulation features, not shaft speed or blade count. Relative energies remove ordinary gain dependence; clipping and zero fractions retain digital-quality information. Synthetic checks verify gain invariance and known-band concentration.

Training examples provide a second kind of evidence: resemblance to known source categories. We form one normalized mean feature vector, or prototype, per acoustic-fit source. For each category we measure maximum cosine similarity and mean similarity to the three closest distinct-source prototypes. The maxima and means are centred across classes; a softmax with temperature 0.1 supplies five further values, giving 15 features. This prevents a repeatedly recorded source from dominating the comparison. Query-source prototypes are excluded. Similarity is a statistical analogy, not physical proof of a vessel’s identity.

The full model concatenates 37 acoustic, 62 physical and 15 exemplar values into a 114-dimensional vector. Weighted standardization and multinomial logistic regression are fitted on policy-fit only. The sample weight is proportional to 1/(Gᶜ nᵍ), where Gᶜ is the number of recorded sources in class c and nᵍ the number of policy-fit clips from source g; weights are normalized to mean one. This gives equal total fitting mass to each class and each source within class. All refits share C ∈ {0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10}, a maximum of 2,000 optimizer iterations and development macro-F1 selection. A scalar probability temperature minimizes development negative log-likelihood after model selection.

The full model selects C=0.01. Baselines include the single anchor, three-model ensemble, acoustic-only refit, physical-only refit and prototype voting. Ablations remove the physical features or prototypes while retaining the same fit labels, standardization, weighting and C search. A pre-freeze diagnostic also tested fixed mixtures of each refit with the ensemble at weights 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1; none improved the ensemble’s development macro-F1. Those mixtures were not adopted. The full fusion remains the proposed method even when a baseline or ablation is stronger.

4. Review Decisions, Generative Comparisons and Statistics

The referral score asks whether the most likely category is supported consistently. Let p be the fused category probabilities, pᴹ the measured-feature model and pᴿ the prototype model. We use s = max(p) − γ[JSD(p,pᴹ) + JSD(p,pᴿ)]/2, where Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) measures disagreement on a 0–1 scale. Development chooses γ from {0,0.25,0.5,1} by accuracy among its top 70% ranked recordings; γ=0.25 is selected. Setting γ=0 tests confidence alone with identical category predictions. The score ranks answers for review; it is not a guaranteed probability of correctness.

For each method, the calibration threshold is the score of the ceil(0.70×508)=356th highest calibration recording, including ties. This fixes a deployable rule before evaluating fresh recordings; it does not guarantee 70% reporting on a shifted cohort. We count correct issued labels, wrong issued labels and referrals over every requested recording. A referral never counts as a correct identification. Separately, exact test-ranked reporting curves compare equal numbers of issued labels; these curves are descriptive and do not supply deployment thresholds. Vessel/background classification sums the four vessel probabilities and uses a development-selected binary threshold, without introducing event detection or false alerts per hour.

The frozen Qwen2.5-Omni-7B Thinker [13] supplies three supplementary identification conditions: original audio alone; audio with the anchor probabilities; and audio with fused probabilities, broad-band relative energies, received-energy change, clipping fraction and prototype class analogies. Every condition uses the same category-only prompt, greedy decoding, a 128-token limit, one call and a JSON category plus one short reason. The native processor receives anti-aliased 16 kHz audio. A development-only format pilot returns 20 valid records from 20 requests. No query labels or provenance identifiers enter these prompts. No human-like skill is imputed to the model.

All three generative conditions evaluate every fresh clean recording, yielding 705 requested category answers. One deterministic JSON extraction is permitted, with no second generation or content repair. Truncated, malformed and uncertain outputs remain in the denominator as withheld/failed identifications. For prior-assisted conditions, we count previously correct numeric classifications changed to an incorrect or withheld answer, and previously wrong classifications corrected by generation. The proposed operator record keeps the numeric classifier’s category authoritative; a generated interpretation is supplementary and cannot be credited with improving identification merely by repeating that category.

Before fresh test inference, an internal record at 2026-11-16T04:26:06.382609+00:00 freezes cohort, code, prompts, model hashes and decision rules. The prior study’s findings were already known; this is not an external preregistration. The three-model ensemble is the primary comparator because it has the highest development macro-F1. Macro-F1 averages a precision–recall score across the five categories, giving each equal weight. The primary endpoint scores all fresh clips before referral. Success requires a gain of at least 0.03 with a positive lower bound on the paired source-bootstrap 95% interval.

Uncertainty uses 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of recorded source groups, retaining all clips from each sampled group and keeping fitted parameters and thresholds fixed. A UTC-day-cluster sensitivity addresses shared-day dependence. We report accuracy, class recalls and confusion counts alongside macro-F1. Per-class, ablation, generative and degradation findings are exploratory; they do not replace the single primary criterion. Six fixed transformations—Gaussian noise at 20, 10 and 0 dB SNR, a 2 kHz low-pass, gain 0.25 and RMS-scaled digital clipping—are applied to all 235 fresh recordings. Every condition receives new acoustic features and classifier predictions without retraining. Nominal category labels remain unchanged, but recognizability after severe distortion is not independently annotated.

5. Identification Results and Review Tradeoffs

The full fusion correctly identifies 142/235 recordings (60.4%), compared with 130/235 (55.3%) for the single anchor, 132/235 (56.2%) for the matched acoustic-only refit and 146/235 (62.1%) for the locked ensemble. Table 2 separates full-cohort identification from automatically issued labels. The primary macro-F1 difference is -0.3 points, with interval -7.3 to 6.6; the UTC-day interval is -7.9 to 7.1. The prospective identification success criterion was not met. The interval does not establish either superiority or equivalence to the ensemble.

The class-level tradeoff is substantial (Fig. 1). Fusion identifies 31/50 tankers, compared with 9/50 for the ensemble, but only 25/50 cargo vessels versus 40/50. The paired recall changes are +44.0 points for tanker (pointwise 95% interval +28.0 to +60.0) and −30.0 for cargo (−48.0 to −12.0). The matched acoustic-only refit also identifies 31 tankers, so that tanker improvement cannot be attributed specifically to the added measurements. Relative to that matched refit, fusion gains five correct background labels and six tug/tow labels, loses one cargo label, and leaves passenger/tanker totals unchanged. These are exploratory category comparisons, not simultaneous confidence claims.

Removing measured acoustic features reduces macro-F1 from 0.583 to 0.517: an exploratory gain of 6.6 points (pointwise interval 0.7 to 12.5). Against the matched acoustic-only model, the gain is 3.7 points (-1.4 to 8.9). Removing prototypes gives 0.582. This separates two design choices: measured sound characteristics contribute within the full system, while the added prototype comparisons provide little further improvement here. Such component tests identify which evidence merits further development.

At the frozen reporting thresholds, fusion issues 132 labels: 96 correct and 36 wrong, referring 103 recordings. The ensemble issues 153: 102 correct and 51 wrong, referring 82. Accuracy among issued labels is 72.7% versus 66.7%, but fusion returns fewer labels. At the same descriptive count of 165 issued labels, fusion gives 115 correct and 50 wrong versus the ensemble’s 110 and 55. The confidence-only fusion ablation also gives 115 and 50 at that count. This run therefore provides no additional benefit for the disagreement term at that matched reporting point. These rankings are not frozen deployment thresholds.

Broad vessel/background balanced accuracy is 92.5% for fusion and 93.5% for the ensemble on the five-category cohort. This easier distinction must not be presented as five-class object-identification accuracy. A prototype-channel permutation changes 101 category predictions, converting 55 correct labels to errors and correcting 28 errors; accuracy falls to 48.9%. That intervention demonstrates reliance on the fitted evidence channels, while the no-prototype ablation shows that reliance is not itself proof of an incremental benefit.

6. Degraded Recordings and Untrained Categories

Added noise substantially reduces identification accuracy. At 20 dB SNR, fusion reaches 48.5% versus 44.7% for the ensemble; at 10 dB, 35.7% versus 30.6%; at 0 dB, 22.6% versus 22.1%. These are exploratory stress outcomes. Gain 0.25 leaves the category predictions unchanged, consistent with relative-energy normalization. Low-pass filtering reduces fusion to 49.8%, below the ensemble’s 54.9%; the physical-only classifier falls to 22.1%. The explicit acoustic features are therefore not uniformly robust to loss of the upper frequency range. Digital clipping changes accuracy only slightly in this particular transformation and cohort; this does not establish tolerance to arbitrary clipping.

The noise test reveals a consequential design finding: willingness to answer can increase as evidence quality falls. Fusion’s reporting rate rises from 56.2% on clean recordings to 87.2% when added noise and signal have equal power (0 dB signal-to-noise ratio, SNR). Of 205 issued labels, only 50 are correct: 24.4% accuracy. Thus a threshold set on clean recordings does not reliably identify when noisy inputs need review. For acoustic assistance, this motivates a separate recording-quality check instead of interpreting confidence alone as permission to answer.

7. Agentic Decisions and Evidence-Conditioned Generation

Figure 3 makes the agent’s contribution observable. In the first study, the learned controller returns the same 37 supported details as the LLM planner with 99 fewer queries and 20 fewer wrong/unsupported details. Against the locked heuristic it also loses 16 supported details. Report-retention thresholds differ, and the original success criterion was not met. The result identifies a central design tradeoff: selective evidence gathering can reduce query counts and wrong statements while also reducing completeness. The separate fresh identification study uses fixed evidence in one generator call.

On the fresh cohort, all 705 generated outputs parse. Audio-only prompting withholds all 235 categories; this describes the fixed prompt/decoder, not general model competence. Acoustic-prior and fused-evidence prompting each identify 99/235 correctly (42.1%), while wrong labels fall from 68 to 44 and withheld labels rise from 68 to 92. Macro-F1 is 0, 0.411 and 0.456 for the three conditions, counting withheld labels as missed identifications. Both the supplied posterior and measured evidence change, so the comparison does not isolate the measurements’ effect.

8. Discussion, Reproducibility and Conclusion

Three findings guide acoustic-assistant design: explicit sound measurements contribute within this system while prototypes add little; selective acquisition and generated withholding trade errors against completeness; and review rules calibrated on clean recordings fail under severe noise and unfamiliar categories. These findings motivate tests of evidence selection, recording-quality checks and category-preserving explanations.

Fresh sources share the earlier test’s collection and recording days, while fitting/selection/calibration dates remain disjoint. Missing parent/deployment provenance precludes complete physical-independence claims. Dataset labels are not expert judgments of every audible event. Passenger and some unfamiliar-category source counts are small. Five-second clips lack contact history; overlapping events are not explicitly modeled. Artificial noise does not establish perceptual recognizability after severe distortion, and the sampled category mix does not represent traffic prevalence. Source-weighting and representation effects are not fully isolated.

Identification uses dataset labels without human reviewers. Earlier report-support checks used numerical proxies and two correlated AI reviewer roles, not identification ground truth. Generated reasons lack independent validation; parsing does not establish factuality. This civilian, single-region study supports neither propulsion-parameter nor submarine-specific recognition claims.

The bundle retains freezes, prompts, fitting searches, model/threshold hashes, confusion matrices, intervals, degradation results and worker timings (32 GB RTX PRO 4500 Blackwell). Inference covers 235 clean and 1,410 transformed clips through three backbones, 705 generations, 20 format checks and 72 unfamiliar-category clips. Audio, models and detailed outputs remain locally outside Git. Portable checks cover weighting, prototypes, accounting, threshold ties and known signals.

This work advances agentic AI for underwater acoustic assistance by connecting inspectable evidence choices to delivered information. On this cohort, fusion improves on the single acoustic model and no-measurement variant without establishing superiority to the ensemble. Fewer generated errors accompany increased withholding; noise motivates explicit quality checks. This application-specific methodology supplies a reproducible basis for examining assistants’ evidence, errors and review decisions before deployment. Testing these design choices with operators is the next step.
